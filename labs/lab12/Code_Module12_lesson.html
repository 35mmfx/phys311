1   Always-Running Development

There are two ways to build a feature. One of them works.

Comparison of large-batch development with incremental, test-and-commit development
The way that failsThe way that works
ApproachWrite the whole feature, then run itChange one thing, run it, commit, repeat
When a bug appearsSomewhere in 300 new linesIn the 10 lines you just wrote
Time to find itHoursSeconds
If it's unfixableYou've lost a daygit checkout . and you've lost ten minutes
💡 Your simulation should never be broken for more than about ten minutes. If it has been broken longer than that, stop adding and start subtracting until it runs again. A working simulation is your measuring instrument, you cannot debug physics without one. Being "mid-rewrite" for two days is how a project dies.

1.1   Slicing a feature into always-running steps

Suppose your one new thing is line-segment terrain for a lunar lander. Don't write a terrain system. Write these, in order, running after each:

  1. Draw one hard-coded line segment. Nothing collides. Commit.
  2. Compute closest-point distance from the lander to that segment; print it. Commit.
  3. When distance < radius, print "HIT" once. Physics unchanged. Commit.
  4. On hit, reflect velocity about the segment normal with e = 1. Check it bounces sensibly. Commit.
  5. Apply your real restitution and positional correction. Commit.
  6. Replace the hard-coded segment with an array of segments. Commit.

Six commits, six working states. If step 4 goes wrong, you know with certainty that steps 1–3 are fine and the bug is in the reflection math, which is fifteen lines.

🔬 Notice step 3. Detection is validated before response exists. This is the broad-phase / narrow-phase / resolution separation from Week 9, used as a debugging strategy. Most collision bugs students report turn out to be detection bugs misdiagnosed as response bugs, because they wrote both at once.

2   Git as a Safety Net

You've been committing weekly all semester. This week, commit hourly.

💡 Branch before every risky experiment. The reason students hesitate to try the interesting approach is fear of breaking the working one. A branch removes that fear entirely: worst case you delete it and you're exactly where you started. This is the single cheapest confidence upgrade available to you this week.
⚠️ Commit messages matter now. "update", "fix", and "stuff" are useless when you're trying to find the last working state at 11pm. Write what changed: "verlet: fix half-step ordering", "terrain: segments array replaces hardcoded line". You will thank yourself in Week 13, and part of your final grade looks at commit history as evidence of incremental work.

3   Debugging a Physics Simulation

"It looks wrong" is not a bug report. Turn it into one with this sequence.

3.1   The decision tree

Something is wrong.
├─ Is the Console showing an error?
│    → YES: read it. Fix that first. Nothing else matters.
├─ Are any values NaN or Infinity?
│    → YES: go to §3.2 (blow-up). Almost always divide-by-zero or overlap.
├─ Does it break only when the frame rate changes / tab is backgrounded?
│    → YES: your timestep isn't fixed. Re-check the accumulator (Week 8).
├─ Does energy drift when it shouldn't?
│    → YES: go to §3.3 (integration order).
├─ Does it behave correctly with ONE body but not many?
│    → YES: force accumulation or pair iteration. Check clearForces() and i<j loops.
├─ Does it behave correctly with DT halved?
│    → YES: not a bug — an accuracy limit. Lower DT or use a better integrator.
└─ None of the above
     → go to §3.4 (bisection).

3.2   The five classic bugs

Common simulation physics bugs, their likely causes, and recommended fixes
SymptomAlmost alwaysFix
Everything vanishes; values are NaNDivision by zero — two bodies at identical positions, or r = 0 in inverse-square gravitySoftening: r2 + eps2; or skip pairs closer than a floor distance
Bodies jitter or sink into each otherPenetration corrected by velocity only, never by positionPositional correction: push apart along the normal by the overlap before applying impulse
Objects gain energy every bounceCollision resolved more than once per step, or resolved while still separatingOnly resolve if the relative normal velocity is negative (approaching)
Fast body passes through a wallTunneling , it moved more than its own diameter in one stepSmaller DT, thicker walls, or a swept test. Cap maximum speed as a stopgap
Simulation is subtly wrong but stableForce accumulator not cleared, so forces from last step persist and doubleclearForces() at the top of step(), before any force applies
🔬 A NaN is a physics message, not just a crash. It nearly always means a singularity in your force law got reached — two point masses at zero separation is genuinely infinite force. The fix isn't a coding trick, it's admitting your point-particle model breaks down at short range, and choosing a softening length that says where. That's a modeling decision worth one sentence in your write-up.

3.3   When energy drifts

Check in this order — it's roughly the frequency order of real causes:

  1. Is your PE complete? Every conservative force needs a potentialEnergy(). A missing spring PE looks exactly like energy drift.
  2. Is the integrator ordering right? Verlet needs acceleration from the start of the step and from the end. Reusing the old acceleration for both is the single most common port bug.
  3. Are you resolving collisions inside the integrator? Don't. Integrate, then resolve, then measure energy — measuring mid-step gives nonsense.
  4. Is anything dissipative that you forgot about? Drag with a tiny coefficient, or e = 0.99 instead of 1.0.
  5. Is it actually bounded oscillation? Euler-Cromer's energy oscillates by design. Plot it over many periods before calling it drift.

3.4   Bisection: the method that always works

When you have no hypothesis, stop guessing and start halving.

💡 Reduce the scene until the bug disappears. Twenty bodies → two. All forces → one force. Collisions on → off. Your new feature → disabled. Keep simplifying until it behaves correctly, then add back one thing. The thing that reintroduces the bug is the bug. This takes ten minutes and beats an hour of staring at code, every single time.

The same logic works on your history: if it worked yesterday and not today, git log the commits between and check them out one at a time. Six commits means three checkouts to find the culprit.

3.5   Instrument, don't squint

// Put numbers on screen. Guessing from animation is hopeless.
if (DEBUG) {
  hud.line(`E: ${E.toFixed(6)}  (drift ${(100*(E-E0)/E0).toFixed(3)}%)`);
  hud.line(`|p|: ${p.length().toFixed(6)}`);
  hud.line(`L: ${L.toFixed(6)}`);
  hud.line(`contacts this step: ${world.contacts.length}`);
  hud.line(`max |v|: ${maxSpeed.toFixed(2)} m/s`);
}

A contact count that reads 3 when two circles touch tells you instantly that you're resolving the same collision repeatedly, a bug that is essentially invisible on screen.

✍️ Try it: Add a DEBUG flag to your project today, wired to a URL parameter (?debug=1) or the D key. Put energy drift, momentum magnitude, contact count, and max speed behind it. Leave it in your final submission — it's a feature, not scaffolding, and reviewers will use it.

4   Cutting Scope, Deliberately

Cutting a feature is not failure. Cutting it too late is.

4.1   The Wednesday rule

💡 If your MUST tier isn't essentially done by midweek, cut something now. Not "push harder", cut. Move the hardest MUST item down to SHOULD, simplify it, or replace it with the fallback you named in your proposal. A project that ships a smaller MUST tier on time scores far better than one that ships an ambitious tier broken, and you already wrote down what the fallback would be. Trust your Week 11 self.
⚠️ With Week 13 reserved for validation, documentation, and launch, a feature deferred out of Week 12 is deferred out of the project — there is no later week to recover it. Treat the midweek checkpoint as a genuine go/no-go decision, not a status update.

4.2   How to cut cleanly

Recommended practices for managing experimental features and documenting implementation tradeoffs
Do thisNot this
Move the code to a branch and note it in DECISIONS.mdDelete it in frustration at midnight
Replace with the simplest thing that works (stiff spring instead of rigid rod)Leave it half-implemented and crashing
Put it behind a flag, off by defaultShip it on by default and hope nobody clicks it
Write one sentence in your write-up about the tradeoffPretend it was never in the plan
🔬 Documented tradeoffs earn credit. "I replaced the rigid constraint with a stiff spring (k = 5000 N/m); this introduces a small oscillation at the constraint but keeps the system stable at DT = 1/240, and energy drift stays under 0.4%" is a good sentence. It shows you understood the physics of your own compromise. Silently shipping less earns nothing; explaining why you shipped less earns most of it back.

4.3   The DECISIONS.md file

Start one now. Each entry is three lines: what you were going to do, what you did instead, why. It takes thirty seconds and it writes half of your reflection for you.

## 2026-11-18 — Constraint approach
Planned: rigid distance constraint solved iteratively.
Did: stiff spring, k = 5000, with damping c = 20.
Why: spike showed constraint drift growing past 3% length error
     over 60 s; spring is stable and visually indistinguishable.

5   Getting Unstuck Fast

You have limited weeks left. Being stuck for six hours is a scope decision you didn't intend to make.

💡 The 45-minute rule. Stuck on the same bug for 45 minutes with no new information? Post it. Not "still trying", post. In an async course the discussion forum is your lab bench, and a well-formed question typically gets answered in a few hours. Six hours of solo grinding costs you a feature.

5.1   A question that gets answered

Information to include in a reproducible physics bug report, with examples
IncludeExample
What you expected"Two equal masses, e = 1, head-on: velocities should swap."
What happened, numerically"Both end up at −1.4 m/s; |Δp| = 2.8 kg·m/s."
The smallest reproducing case"Two bodies, no gravity, no drag. Scene in main.js lines 40–52."
What you already ruled out"Detection fires exactly once (contact count = 1). Masses are correct in the HUD."
A linkYour Pages URL or a permalink to the commit

Notice that writing this out often solves the problem before you hit post. That's not a bug in the method, it's the main feature.

✍️ Try it — the rubber duck commit. When you're stuck, write your bug report as a commit message on a scratch branch before posting it. Forcing the expected-vs-actual comparison into words resolves a surprising fraction of physics bugs, because "should swap" versus "both went the same direction" immediately implicates the relative-velocity sign.

Deployment bugs (case-sensitive filenames, absolute paths, missing .js extensions, browser caching) are a known category from Week 10 , and they only appear on the live site, never on Live Server. Push a deploy on day one, even if the simulation is barely started. Then every later push is verified continuously and the last day contains no surprises.
💡 What "done" means this week. Someone clicks your public link on a phone, sees your simulation running, interacts with it, and the physics is correct. That's it. No polish, no second scene, no documentation. Get there, and Week 13 becomes about making it good rather than making it exist.
