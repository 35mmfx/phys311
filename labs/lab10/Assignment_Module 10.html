Your engine works and it's public. This week you give it a user. You'll add mouse and keyboard interaction, a real control panel, visual design that communicates physics, and the accessibility and documentation that turn a lab into publishable work.

💡 The big idea this week: Interactivity isn't decoration — it's a form of physics communication. A slider that lets someone sweep restitution from 1 to 0 and watch energy drain teaches inelastic collisions faster than a paragraph can. Every control you add should let a user discover something. Controls that don't reveal physics are clutter.

Part A — Input & Control Panel (30 points)

GUIDED — Follow the Lesson

A1 — Coordinate-correct input (10 pts)

  1. Implement eventToWorld() with the getBoundingClientRect ratio correction from the lesson.
  2. Prove it's correct: display the cursor's physics coordinates live on the HUD, and draw a crosshair at that world position. Click each of the four canvas corners and confirm the readouts match your world bounds. Include a screenshot.
  3. Use pointer* events so touch works. Verify on a phone if you can.

A2 — Simulation controls (12 pts)

Implement all of the following as real HTML elements, each with a working keyboard equivalent:

A3 — Parameter sliders (8 pts)

  1. At least three sliders that change physics live, each with a numeric readout that updates as you drag (e.g. gravity, restitution, drag coefficient, spring stiffness, mass).
  2. Clamp every range to stable values and state in your write-up how you chose the upper bound for at least one slider — referencing the stability condition from the lesson.
  3. Parse all values with parseFloat.
⚠️ Single-step is the control graders check most carefully. Pressing it must advance exactly one DT — not one frame, not "a little bit." If t on your HUD doesn't increase by precisely your DT each press, your accumulator handling is wrong, and that same bug is silently affecting your normal runs too.

Part B — Direct Manipulation & Visual Physics (35 points)

GUIDED → INDEPENDENT

B1 — Manipulate bodies (15 pts)

Implement both:

  1. Grab and drag using the MouseSpring approach — a damped spring toward the cursor, added as a Force. Bodies must still collide correctly while held, and releasing must impart a physical velocity.
  2. Slingshot launch — press to place, drag to aim, release to launch, with a live trajectory preview drawn while aiming.

Then answer in your write-up: Why does teleport-dragging (setting pos directly) break the physics? Name two specific things that go wrong. Try it briefly and describe what you observed.

B2 — Visuals that reveal physics (12 pts)

Implement at least three, each individually toggleable:

  • Motion trails with alpha fade
  • Velocity vectors (scaled arrows)
  • Force vectors (drawn before forces are cleared)
  • Color mapped to speed or kinetic energy, with a legend
  • Collision flash / contact-point markers
  • Live energy strip chart on a secondary canvas

For each one, write one sentence in your write-up naming what a viewer can now understand that they couldn't before. A visual that doesn't earn that sentence doesn't earn the points.

B3 — Responsive & accessible (8 pts)

  1. Canvas resizes with the window, handles devicePixelRatio, and keeps the world in view.
  2. Fully keyboard operable — every control reachable by Tab and activatable without a mouse.
  3. Proper <label for> on every input; meaning never conveyed by color alone; fallback text inside the <canvas> element.
  4. Run the unplugged-mouse test from the lesson and report the result honestly.

INDEPENDENT

Requirements

  1. A clear purpose, stated on the page itself in one or two sentences — a visitor should know what they're looking at in under 15 seconds.
  2. Physics from at least two different weeks of Blocks 1–2. Name them in your write-up.
  3. Conservation diagnostics remain visible (a "show diagnostics" toggle is fine, but the honesty must be available).
  4. Validated: report one quantitative conservation result for your final scene — energy drift over 60 s, or momentum conservation through N collisions.
  5. Robust: it must survive aggressive use. Spam every button, max every slider, drag bodies into walls at speed, resize mid-run. Nothing should produce NaN, a frozen frame, or a Console error.
  6. Deployed and working on GitHub Pages, tested on both a laptop and a phone.
⚠️ Scope discipline. One polished, robust interaction beats five half-finished ones every time — in this rubric and in the final project rubric. If you're running out of time, cut features and polish what remains. "It has a lot of buttons but three of them crash it" scores far below "it does one thing flawlessly."

Students who build something here that they actually enjoy spend Weeks 11–14 refining instead of restarting — and their final projects are consistently stronger. Peer review structure (required for full credit)

Open their link cold — no questions first. Then answer:

  • 15-second test: Could you tell what it does and how to interact, without being told? Quote what tipped you off, or what was missing.
  • Do the controls do what they say? Try each one. Note any mismatch between label and behavior.
  • Try to break it. Max every slider, spam reset, drag a body into a wall as fast as you can, resize the window while running. Report exactly what you did and what happened.
  • Physics check: Does it look right? Do the diagnostics support the claim? Does anything violate a conservation law you can see?
  • One specific strength and one specific, actionable improvement. "Nice work" and "add more features" earn no credit; "the velocity arrows made the collision suddenly legible" and "the restitution slider goes to 1.2, which injects energy — clamp it at 1.0" do.

Peer reviews are worth 10 of this assignment's 100 points and are graded on specificity.

⭐ Bonus — Choose One (up to 10 points)

  • Shareable state URLs (10 pts). Encode the current scene parameters into the URL query string and restore from it on load, so a user can share an exact configuration. Include a "copy link" button and demonstrate with two distinct URLs.
  • Side-by-side integrator race (10 pts). Two canvases running identical initial conditions with different integrators, plus a live plot of the divergence between them. This is your Week 2 lesson as a public exhibit.
  • Data export (10 pts). A "download CSV" button dumping the recorded time series (t, positions, energies) so a visitor can analyze your simulation in Python — closing the loop back to Block 1.
