1   The Validation Report

Your final submission includes a validation section. It is worth more than any other single piece of the write-up. It feels like homework about work they've already done.  It isn't.  It is the evidence that the work means anything.

1.1   What a validation claim looks like

Quantitative validation of energy conservation, pendulum period accuracy, timestep convergence, and momentum conservation in collision simulations
Not validationValidation
"Energy is conserved.""Conservative mode, Verlet, DT = 1/240: |ΔE/E₀| = 0.08% over 300 s (measured, n = 72,000 steps). Target was < 0.5%."
"The pendulum period matches theory.""At θ₀ = 0.05 rad, measured T = 2.0071 s vs. 2π√(L/g) = 2.0064 s — 0.03% high, consistent with the expected +θ₀²/16 correction of 0.016%."
"It's stable at small timesteps.""Halving DT from 1/120 → 1/240 → 1/480 changed landing speed by 0.41% then 0.10% — ratio 4.1, consistent with second-order convergence."
"Collisions conserve momentum.""Over 500 randomized two-body collisions at e ∈ [0, 1]: max |Δp|/|p₀| = 3.1×10⁻¹⁵; KE never increased (max ΔKE = −1.2×10⁻¹⁶ J)."
💡 The pattern: quantity, condition, number, comparison. Every claim names what was measured, under what conditions, the actual value, and what it should have been. If any of the four is missing, it isn't a validation claim — it's a hope.

1.2   Four tests, and which apply to you

Recommended validation tests for dynamical simulations, when to apply them, and the quantities to report
TestApplies whenWhat to report
ConservationAlways — use conservative mode if your system is dissipativeDrift as % of E₀ over a stated duration; also |Δp| and L if relevant
Analytic limitAlmost always — some limit of your system probably has a formulaMeasured vs. formula, with % error and the reason for any residual

1.3   Running a convergence study on a real-time simulation

Your simulation runs at 60 fps with user input, which isn't a controlled experiment. The fix is a headless mode — and this is where the Week 9 layering rule finally pays for itself completely.

// convergence.js — no canvas, no rendering, pure physics.
// Possible ONLY because nothing in engine/ mentions ctx or document.
import { buildScene } from "./scene.js";

function runTo(tEnd, dt) {
  const world = buildScene();          // identical initial conditions
  const n = Math.round(tEnd / dt);
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) world.step(dt);
  return world;                        // measure whatever you like
}

for (const dt of [1/60, 1/120, 1/240, 1/480]) {
  const w = runTo(20.0, dt);
  console.log(dt.toFixed(5), w.bodies[0].pos.y.toFixed(8), w.energy().toFixed(8));
}

Treat the smallest DT as your reference, compute error against it, and check the ratio. Halving DT should shrink the error by ~4× for a second-order method (Euler-Cromer, Verlet) and ~2× for first-order Euler.

🔬 If your convergence order is wrong, you have a bug — and this test just found it. Verlet measuring first-order almost always means the acceleration ordering is wrong (reusing the old acceleration for the second half-step). A method that doesn't converge at all usually means something in your loop depends on wall-clock time rather than DT. This is the most sensitive bug detector in the course, and it's why the test is required rather than optional.
✍️ Try it: Run your headless convergence script before you do anything else this week. It takes twenty minutes to write and it will either confirm your integrator is correct or hand you a specific, findable bug. Either outcome is worth having on Monday rather than Friday.

1.4   Reporting honestly

💡 A limitation you state costs you nothing.  "Energy drifts up to 1.8% during multi-body pileups because I resolve contacts sequentially rather than simultaneously; single and pairwise collisions stay under 0.02%" is a strong sentence.  It shows you measured the failure mode, understand its cause, and bounded it.  Silence on the same issue reads as either not knowing or hoping nobody checks.

Every real simulation has limits. Yours should list them: where the model breaks down, what DT range is valid, what body count degrades performance, what physics you deliberately left out.

2.2   The fifteen-second test

A visitor should learn three things without scrolling or reading carefully:

  1. What am I looking at? — one sentence, above or beside the canvas
  2. What do I do? — visible controls, or a one-line prompt ("Drag the planet to launch it")
  3. What should I notice? — the physics payoff ("Watch the energy readout as you increase drag")
⚠️ The most common documentation failure is assuming your controls are obvious. They are obvious to you because you wrote them. If a control isn't labeled on the page, it doesn't exist.

3   Visuals That Communicate Physics

You've had this rule since Week 10: every visual element should carry information. Week 13 is when you apply it ruthlessly and remove what doesn't.

Visual elements in physics simulations, what they communicate, and best practices for presenting them
ElementCommunicatesDo it well by…
TrailsHistory — orbit shape, chaos divergenceFading opacity; length tied to a physical time, not a frame count
Force / velocity arrowsVector quantities that are otherwise invisibleConsistent scale, stated in a legend; toggleable
ColorA scalar field — speed, energy, massOne meaning per channel; never color alone as the only cue
SizeMass or radius — if it's physicalSay whether radius is to scale; note if it isn't
Live plot / HUDConserved quantities and driftFixed axes so the eye can compare over time
Grid & scale barUnits — the difference between "a picture" and "a measurement"A labeled 1 m (or 1 AU) bar. Cheap, and hugely credibility-building
💡 The subtraction pass. Go through your renderer and ask of every drawn element: if I deleted this, would the viewer understand less physics? If no, delete it. Glow effects, particle sparkles, and gradient backgrounds usually fail this test. A clean scene with a scale bar and honest arrows looks more professional than a busy one.

4   Peer Review

You posted your beta at the end of Week 12 and hopefully received feedback.  This week you review two classmates' projects by December 2

4.1   Reviewing well

Examples contrasting vague, low-actionability reviews with specific, actionable feedback on usability, physics, and accessibility.
Weak reviewUseful review
"Looks great!""I didn't realize I could drag the planet until I read your README — add a one-line prompt on the canvas."
"Maybe add sound?""Your energy drift hit 4% within 20 s when I stacked five bodies — worth checking whether contacts are resolved more than once."
"The physics seems right.""I set restitution to 1.0 and KE still dropped ~2% per bounce, which shouldn't happen — possible positional-correction leak."
"Nice interface.""Tab order skips the integrator dropdown, so I couldn't reach it by keyboard."
💡 Try to break their physics. The most valuable review you can give is not aesthetic. Crank a slider to its extreme. Stack twenty bodies. Set drag to zero and watch energy. Set DT high. Reviewers who stress-test find real bugs, and finding one for a classmate in Week 13 is worth more to them than any compliment.

4.2   Receiving well

You'll get more feedback than you can act on. Triage it:

  1. Physics errors — fix these, always, first.
  2. "I didn't understand what to do" — fix these; they're cheap and they're the difference between a project people use and one they close.
  3. Accessibility gaps — fix these; usually a small change.
  4. Feature suggestions — almost always decline. It's the last week. Note them in your write-up as future work, which is a legitimate and professional response.
🔬 Write down what you did with the feedback.  "Two reviewers couldn't find the launch control, so I added an on-canvas prompt and a labeled button" is evidence of a real iteration cycle — and iteration in response to users is a professional skill. A declined suggestion with a one-line reason counts just as much as an accepted one.

5  The Public Showcase

Your final project goes on a public Course Showcase page linked from the department site — visible to faculty, to students considering this course, and to anyone you send it to.

5.1   What you submit for it

  • Title — a real one. "Mare Tranquillitatis: a lunar lander with terrain collision" beats "Final Project."
  • One-sentence description for a general audience
  • A still image — a clean screenshot, 1200×675 or similar, showing the simulation at its most legible moment
  • Your live URL, your repo URL, and your presentation link
  • One validation number you're willing to stand behind in public (e.g. "ΔE/E₀ < 0.1% over 300 s")
🔬 That last item is the course's signature. Most student project galleries show pictures. Ours shows pictures with a stated accuracy. A visitor who scrolls the Showcase should come away understanding that every one of these was measured, not just made. That is the thing worth being known for.
🔒 Your privacy is yours to control. You may use a display name instead of your full name, and you may ask to have your project listed without your name at all. If you would rather not appear on the public page, tell me — you can submit everything privately in Canvas with no grade penalty whatsoever. Participation in the Showcase is encouraged, not required.
