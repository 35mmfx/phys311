This week you decide what you're building for the rest of the semester. There is less code than usual and more thinking — which most students find harder. A good proposal makes the next three weeks calm; a vague one makes them frantic.

💡 The big idea this week: Scope is a skill. Anyone can list features; the discipline is deciding which features you will cheerfully abandon before you start. Your MUST tier is a promise, your COULD tier is a wish list, and knowing the difference is what gets projects finished.

Part A — The Proposal (55 points)

GUIDED — Follow the Template

One page. Seven sections. Use these exact headings so peer reviewers and I can find things.

A1 — Title & pitch (5 pts)

A title, and one sentence stating what a visitor sees and what they do. No jargon.

💡 Examples of good and bad pitches

Weak: "An interactive physics simulation demonstrating various mechanical principles." — says nothing.

Strong: "Drag a planet into orbit around a binary star and see how long you can keep it stable — the HUD shows energy and angular momentum drifting as the orbit decays."

Strong: "A double pendulum you can release from any angle, side by side with a second one released 0.001 rad away, so you can watch chaos separate them."

A2 — The physics (12 pts)

  1. State every force and constraint in the system, with equations.
  2. Name your one new thing — the physics that isn't already in your Lab 10 engine.
  3. Name the integrator you'll use and justify it (Verlet for orbits and oscillators; Euler-Cromer where dissipation dominates).
  4. State your units and typical scales. A scene in meters and seconds behaves very differently from one in AU and years.

A3 — Three-tier scope (12 pts)

MUST / SHOULD / COULD, as bulleted lists. Rules that will be enforced:

  • MUST is due end of Week 12 and must include "deployed to GitHub Pages"
  • MUST must be small enough that you'd bet money on it
  • COULD must contain at least two items you're prepared to drop

A4 — Validation plan (14 pts)

The highest-value section. Give at least two specific numerical tests. Each must state the quantity, the expected result, and a tolerance.

Examples of vague simulation requirements and their measurable, quantitative alternatives.
Not acceptableAcceptable
"I'll make sure energy is conserved.""Thrust and drag off, Verlet at DT=1/240: |ΔE/E₀| < 0.3% over 120 s."
"I'll check it against theory.""Small-amplitude pendulum period within 1% of 2π√(L/g) for θ₀ = 0.05 rad."
"It should look realistic.""Halving DT from 1/120 to 1/240 changes measured landing speed by < 0.5%."
"Momentum will be conserved in collisions.""Two-body collision at e = 0.6: |Δp|/|p₀| < 10⁻⁹, and KE strictly decreases."

If your system is dissipative or driven, include the "switch it off" test from the lesson — the conservative reduction of your system, with its expected result.

A5 — Biggest risk + fallback (6 pts)

One sentence: "The thing most likely to sink this project is ______, and my fallback is ______." The fallback must be a real, shippable alternative — not "work harder."

A7 — Audience (2 pts)

One sentence: who is this for, and what will they understand after two minutes that they didn't before?

Part B — The 90-Minute Risk Spike (25 points)

INDEPENDENT

Build the riskiest piece of your project as a disposable prototype. Ugly is fine. Broken is acceptable — as long as you report what you learned.

What to do

  1. Identify the risk you named in A5.
  2. Spend ~90 minutes building the smallest thing that tests it. One body, one force, one collision — no UI, no polish, no integration into your engine.
  3. Commit it to project/spike/ regardless of outcome.
  4. Write 150–300 words in project/spike/FINDINGS.md: what you tried, what happened, and what it means for your scope. If it failed, state explicitly whether you're changing the plan or moving the feature to COULD.
💡 A failed spike earns full credit. This is graded on whether you tested the risk and drew a conclusion, not on whether the code works. Discovering in Week 11 that rotational collision response is harder than you thought is a win worth 25 points. Discovering it in Week 13 costs you the project.

Part C — Proposal Discussion (20 points)

Post your proposal to the discussion by midweek, then review at least one peer proposal. Reviews are graded on usefulness, not politeness.

For a peer review, answer all four

  1. Scope check. Is the MUST tier genuinely achievable in one week? If not, name the specific item to cut or move.
  2. Validation check. Are the tests specific and numerical? If either is vague, propose a concrete replacement with a tolerance.
  3. Physics check. Is the "one new thing" actually new, and is it actually one thing? Flag hidden second features.
  4. The interesting question. Ask one question that would make the project better, something the author probably hasn't considered.
⚠️ "Looks great, good luck!" earns zero. The purpose of this workshop is to catch over-scoped projects before they consume three weeks. The most valuable thing you can do for a classmate is tell them their MUST list is too big.

Project Idea Catalog

You are not required to pick from this list, original ideas are encouraged. These are calibrated to be finishable in three weeks with your existing engine.

🌎 Orbits & gravitation
  • Orbital golf — slingshot a probe past planets to hit a target. New thing: trajectory prediction / ghost path.
  • Binary star sandbox — place a test mass and find stable regions. New thing: Lagrange point visualization.
  • Solar system builder — drag planets in, check Kepler's third law live. New thing: automatic period measurement + log-log plot.
  • Hohmann transfer trainer — burn at the right point to change orbit. New thing: Δv budgeting with fuel.
🔒 Oscillators & chaos
  • Double pendulum chaos viewer — two near-identical initial conditions diverging. New thing: rigid constraints or Lagrangian equations of motion.
  • Coupled oscillator lattice — N masses and springs; excite a normal mode. New thing: normal-mode decomposition display.
  • Resonance explorer — drive a damped oscillator, build the response curve live. New thing: real-time amplitude measurement.
  • Wave on a string — a chain of coupled masses producing standing waves. New thing: many-body spring topology + mode counting.
🎮 Games
  • Lunar lander — thrust, fuel, terrain, landing speed threshold. New thing: variable mass or line-segment terrain.
  • Trebuchet / artillery — hit a target with drag and wind. New thing: constrained linkage or a wind field.
  • Stack & topple — build a tower that must not fall. New thing: rotation and resting contact.
  • Billiards — a full table with spin. New thing: friction and tangential impulse.
  • Magnetic maze — steer a charge with qv×B fields. New thing: velocity-dependent force + field visualization.
🔬 Numerical studies
  • Integrator showdown, live — the same orbit under Euler / Euler-Cromer / Verlet side by side, with drift plotted in real time. New thing: real-time plotting + a 4th-order integrator (RK4 or Yoshida).
  • N-body cluster — watch a self-gravitating cloud virialize. New thing: spatial partitioning or Barnes–Hut.
  • Restitution study — a ball pit where e sweeps 1→0 with an energy-loss ledger. New thing: rigorous dissipation accounting.
  • Chaos in the driven pendulum — live Poincaré section. New thing: stroboscopic sampling + phase-space rendering.
⭐ Bonus (+5):  One Peer review is assigned to you.  Do an additional peer review.
