Week 11 -- Scoping a Physics Project

🎯 By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Choose a final project whose physics is the point, not decoration
  • Scope a three-week build realistically, using a must-have / should-have / could-have split
  • Write a validation plan for a system that has no exact solution
  • Identify which new physics your engine needs and where it plugs in
  • Estimate risk and name your single biggest unknown before you start coding
  • Write a proposal that a peer can give useful feedback on
💡 Block 4 has no new mechanics. You already have every tool you need: three integrators, four force types, a collision pipeline, a renderer, an input system, and a public deployment. What Block 4 teaches is harder to grade and more valuable long-term — choosing a problem, scoping it honestly, validating it without an answer key, and explaining it to strangers. That is what practicing computational physicists actually spend their time on.

1   What Makes a Good Project

The single most common failure mode in a project like this isn't bad code. It's a project whose physics turns out to be trivial once it's running — a game where the "physics" is a sprite moving at constant velocity until it hits a wall. It looks finished and teaches nothing.

💡 The test: could you write a validation section about it? If your project has a conserved quantity to track, a known limit to compare against, or a measurable relationship to plot, the physics is real. If the only thing you could write is "it looks right," pick something else. Physics you can validate is physics worth simulating.

1.1   Three shapes a project can take

Comparison of interactive physics project types, their strengths, and key design risks
TypeWhat it isGraded strengthWatch out for
Explanatory simulationA tool that lets a user discover a phenomenon (resonance explorer, orbit sandbox, coupled oscillators)Physics depth; easiest to validateCan feel like a lab plot with sliders — needs real interaction design
Physics gameA goal, a challenge, a win condition (lunar lander, slingshot orbital golf, trebuchet)Engaging; strong public appealGame design eats your time; physics gets "tuned" until it's fake
Numerical studyAn investigation rendered live (chaos in the double pendulum, N-body stability, Lagrange points)Highest physics ceilingCan end up with nothing for a user to do

All three are fully acceptable. The rubric rewards the same things in each: correct physics, honest validation, clean architecture, and a page a stranger can use.

1.2   The "one new thing" rule

Your Lab 10 engine already does uniform gravity, drag, springs, Newtonian gravity, circle collisions, and restitution. A good project adds exactly one substantial new piece of physics on top of that.

Candidate physics features, their implementation difficulty, and where they integrate into the existing system
Candidate "one new thing"DifficultyWhere it plugs in
Constraint: rigid rod / pendulum linkageMediumNew Constraint applied after integration
Coupled oscillator lattice (springs between N bodies)LowLoop of existing Spring forces
Thrust + finite fuel (variable mass)MediumNew Force + body mass changes
Rotation & torque on collision (friction, spin)HighExtend collision resolution with tangential impulse
Polygon or line-segment collision (ramps, walls)HighNew narrow-phase test
Magnetic / velocity-dependent force (qv×B)LowNew Force class, ~10 lines
Buoyancy + fluid drag layerLowNew Force keyed on depth
⚠️ Two new things is the classic over-scope. "Rigid-body rotation and polygon collision and a level editor" is a semester, not three weeks.  Pick one, do it properly, validate it, and spend the leftover time on polish.

2   Scoping: The Three-Tier Plan

Every proposal must split the work into three tiers. This is standard practice in software projects, and it is the single most useful planning tool in this block.

✅ MUST have — If these aren't done, the project fails. This is your Week 12 target, and it should feel almost boringly achievable. Physics correct, one scene, runs without crashing, deployed.
🔁 SHOULD have — What makes it good rather than merely working. Controls, a second scene, the validation HUD, documentation. This is your Week 13 target.
✨ COULD have — Stretch goals you will cheerfully abandon. Sound, level editor, extra scenes, mobile gestures. Touch these only in Week 13, and only if everything above is done.
💡 Write the MUST tier so you'd bet money on finishing it in one week. If your MUST list makes you nervous, it's a SHOULD list in disguise. Deadlines are met by projects with humble MUST tiers and generous COULD tiers, because the COULD tier is the part that's allowed to be cut without anything breaking.

2.1   A worked example

Suppose the project is Lunar Lander with real orbital gravity.

Prioritized feature tiers for the lander game: must-have, should-have, and optional features.
TierItems
MUSTLander body with thrust force and finite fuel; uniform gravity; ground collision with a crash/land speed threshold; keyboard control; energy + fuel HUD; deployed to Pages
SHOULDSwitch to inverse-square gravity from a planet center; rotation & angular thrust; terrain of line segments; validation panel showing momentum bookkeeping under thrust; restart & scoring
COULDFuel-optimal trajectory hint; multiple landing pads; sound; touch controls
🔬 Notice where the physics depth lives. The MUST tier is honest but shallow (uniform gravity). The interesting physics — inverse-square gravity, torque, variable mass — sits in SHOULD, which is exactly right. You are guaranteed a working submission, and the good physics is the first thing you build once it works, not the thing you gamble on.

3   The Validation Plan

This is the section that separates a physics project from a coding project, and it is worth the most points in the proposal rubric. Your project almost certainly has no closed-form solution — that's why you're simulating it. So how will you know it's right?

You already know the answer. You've used it since Week 2.

3.1   Four validation strategies

Strategies for validating simulations through conservation laws, analytic limits, convergence tests, and symmetry or invariance checks
StrategyHow it worksExample
Conservation lawTrack a quantity that must not change; report drift as a fraction of E₀Total energy in a spring lattice; angular momentum in an orbit
Analytic limitTurn off complexity until a textbook formula applies, then comparePendulum at small angle → T = 2π√(L/g); drag → 0 recovers 45° optimum range
Convergence testHalve DT; the answer should stop changing at the expected orderLanding speed vs. DT; orbital period vs. DT (log-log slope ≈ 2)
Symmetry / invarianceA transformation that shouldn't change the outcome doesn'tMirror the scene → mirrored trajectory; shift origin → identical dynamics
💡 Your proposal must name at least two specific, numerical tests — with the quantity, the expected result, and the tolerance. "I'll check energy" is not a plan. "Undriven, undamped: |ΔE/E₀| < 0.5% over 60 s at DT = 1/240 with Velocity Verlet" is a plan. You can pass or fail it, which is the whole point.

3.2   When your project is intentionally non-conservative

Many good projects have drag, thrust, or inelastic collisions — energy should change. That doesn't exempt you; it just changes the test.

Conservation-law tests for drag, thrust, inelastic collisions, and driven systems
SituationThe correct test
Drag presentΔE must equal −∫Fdrag·v dt (accumulate it and compare)
Thrust presentΔp must equal ∫Fthrust dt; with fuel mass, check the rocket equation
Inelastic collisions (e < 1)Momentum exactly conserved; KE must decrease, never increase
Anything drivenTurn the driving off → the conservative test must pass again
🔬 The "switch it off" move is your most reliable tool. Any dissipative or driven system can be reduced to a conservative one by zeroing a coefficient. That reduced system is testable against Block 1–2 standards. Build a ?test=conservative mode or a checkbox that does exactly this — you'll use it every time something looks wrong for the rest of the project.

4   Naming Your Biggest Risk

Your proposal must include one sentence completing: "The thing most likely to sink this project is ______, and my fallback is ______."

This isn't pessimism theater. Projects fail from unexamined assumptions, and writing the risk down forces you to check it early instead of in Week 13.

Common project risks and fallback strategies for maintaining scope, performance, and schedule
Common riskReasonable fallback
"I've never written polygon collision"Ship with circles and axis-aligned walls; polygons move to COULD
"Rotation might not be stable"Lock rotation off with a flag; project still works as a point-mass sim
"200 bodies may be too slow"Cap N at whatever holds 60 fps; report the measured limit as a finding
"The constraint solver may drift"Swap the rigid rod for a stiff spring; document the tradeoff
"I'm traveling in Week 13"Front-load: finish the SHOULD tier in Week 12
✍️ Try it — the 90-minute spike. Before you submit your proposal, spend 90 minutes building the riskiest piece as a throwaway prototype. Don't make it pretty; don't integrate it. Just find out whether it works. If your one new thing is polygon collision, get a single circle bouncing off a single tilted line segment. If it's rotation, get one square spinning under a torque. This is the highest-value 90 minutes of the entire block — a risk you've touched is a risk you can plan around, and if it fails you still have time to change direction.

5   Reusing Your Engine (and What to Change)

Start your project by copying labs/lab10/ into project/. Then resist the urge to rewrite.

⚠️ Do not start from scratch.  Your engine is validated. Refactor in place, one file at a time, with the simulation running the whole time.

5.1   Where new physics goes

Extension points for adding forces, body types, collision shapes, constraints, and integrators
If your new thing is…Add…Touch nothing else
A new forceA class in engine/forces.js with apply(world) and, if conservative, potentialEnergy(world)✅
A new body typeFields on Body + a draw case in render/renderer.js✅
A new collision shapeA narrow-phase function in engine/collisions.js✅
A constraintA constraints[] array on World, resolved after integrationSmall step() change
A new integratorA class in engine/integrators.js with step(world, dt)✅

If your new physics doesn't fit one of these slots, that's a signal worth taking seriously — either you're about to violate the layering rule, or the feature is bigger than you think. Ask in the discussion before you start cutting into world.js.

💡 The architecture payoff arrives now. This table is why Weeks 7 and 9 spent so long on structure. Adding a magnetic force to a well-layered engine is a 10-line file and one line in main.js. Adding it to a tangled one means editing five files and re-debugging your renderer. The students who took the layering rule seriously are about to have a much better three weeks.

6   Writing the Proposal

Your proposal is one page. Its job is not to impress me — it's to be concrete enough that a peer can find the flaw in it. Vague proposals get useless feedback.

Required sections, lengths, and content for a physics project proposal, including scope, validation, risks, timeline, and audience.
SectionLengthMust contain
Title & one-line pitch1 sentenceWhat a visitor sees and does
The physics1 paragraphEquations, forces, and the one new thing
Three-tier scopeBulletedMUST / SHOULD / COULD, with MUST due Week 12
Validation plan2+ itemsQuantity, expected value, tolerance
Biggest risk + fallback1 sentenceNamed, with a real Plan B
Week-by-week timeline4 rowsWeeks 11–14, one deliverable each
Audience1 sentenceWho this is for and what they'll learn
✍️ Try it — the stranger test. Read your pitch out loud to someone who isn't in this course. If they can't tell you what the user does and what physics is involved, rewrite it. You'll do a version of this in Week 13 for real, so it's worth practicing on a low-stakes audience now.
⚠️ A note on ambition. Be ambitious in your COULD tier, not your MUST tier.  The best-received projects are the ones that do one thing beautifully, a single scene, tight controls, honest numbers, a page that explains itself.  The sprawling projects with five half-finished features are the ones students apologize for during a presentation.  Depth reads as skill, breadth reads as unfinished.
